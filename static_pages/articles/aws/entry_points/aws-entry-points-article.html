Every Way Traffic Can Enter AWS — And How to Prove You Control It

When an auditor asks "list every way someone can enter your AWS environment," they're not asking about your Load Balancer. This is your complete framework for application and administrative entry — SOC 2-ready for 2026.

A
AWS Security Architecture Team 12 min read · SOC 2 Common Criteria 6.0 & 7.0

There's a moment in every SOC 2 audit walkthrough that separates mature security teams from everyone else. The auditor leans forward and asks:

"List every way someone or something can enter your AWS environment."

— Every SOC 2 Auditor, Every Time

The inexperienced engineer opens a browser tab and starts screenshotting Security Groups. The experienced one pulls up a two-plane architecture diagram and starts talking about identity.

That's the gap this article closes.

In 2026, mature security teams don't think in terms of "open ports." They think in terms of planes — the Application (Data) Plane and the Administrative (Management) Plane. If you cannot clearly separate and secure both, your architecture is not audit-ready. Full stop.

◆ PART ONE ◆
Application / Data Plane

The Front Doors: How Users & Systems Reach Your Workloads

Your application plane is your external-facing surface area. Every public user, B2B partner, SaaS integration, and IoT device touches this layer before it touches anything else. But it isn't a single door — it's a series of controlled checkpoints, each with a specific job.

LAYER 01The Public Edge — Your Cloud DMZ

The edge is where the internet meets your architecture. Think of it as the vestibule of a high-security building: you're allowed to enter, but you haven't been let inside yet.

Services in this layer

Amazon Route 53 · CloudFront · AWS WAF · Global Accelerator · AWS Shield

This layer handles DNS routing, TLS termination, CDN caching, DDoS mitigation, web attack filtering (SQLi, XSS), and geo-based restrictions. If this layer fails, everything behind it becomes reachable — which is exactly why auditors scrutinize it first.

🔍

SOC 2 Focus (Security + Availability): Auditors expect WAF managed rule sets enabled, Shield Advanced for high-availability workloads, DNS query logging enabled, and logs shipped to a centralized, immutable destination.

LAYER 02Compute Gateways — Controlled Application Entry

Once traffic clears the edge, it needs to reach your application. The critical compliance principle here is separation: your compute should never be directly internet-facing. Only controlled gateways accept traffic, and those gateways sit in public subnets while everything else stays private.

The SOC 2 Gold Standard Pattern

Public Subnet: ALB / API Gateway → Private Subnet: ECS / EKS / EC2 / Lambda

This separation proves three things simultaneously: no compute is directly internet-reachable, only inspected gateways accept inbound connections, and least-privilege networking is actually enforced — not just claimed.

Required controls at this layer aren't optional decorations — they're audit evidence. TLS 1.2+ enforcement, OAuth2/OIDC authentication, mTLS where sensitive data flows, rate limiting and throttling on APIs, and hardened security groups with documented justifications for every rule.

LAYER 03Private & Hybrid Entry — The "Backdoor" Layer

These paths aren't public-facing, but don't let that comfort you. Site-to-Site VPN, Direct Connect, PrivateLink, and Client VPN are all entry points — and in an audit, "private" doesn't mean "unaccountable."

Zero Trust: AWS Verified Access

AWS Verified Access · AWS Client VPN · PrivateLink · Direct Connect

The paradigm shift that matters most here: a VPN gives users a key to the house. Verified Access checks who you are, what device you're using, whether that device meets your security policy — and it does this check on every single access attempt, not just at login. That's why auditors love it. It operationalizes Zero Trust's core principle: Never Trust. Always Verify.

One compliance detail that carries real weight: Gateway Endpoints (for S3 and DynamoDB) mean your data never crosses the public internet. Interface Endpoints create private ENIs to AWS services within your VPC. When you can say "our data never leaves the AWS backbone," that sentence means something in an audit room.

LAYER 04Cross-Account Entry

Multi-account architectures are the AWS best practice — but they create entry paths that teams often forget to document. Dev accounts, shared services, CI/CD pipelines, staging environments: all of these can have a route into Production if you're not deliberate about isolation.

Auditors will ask you to prove that a compromised Dev account cannot enter Production. Your answer lives in route table isolation, Transit Gateway configuration, and Service Control Policies applied at the Organization level. If you can't show all three, you can't answer the question confidently.

LAYER 05Service-Initiated Entry Points (Commonly Forgotten)

This is where most teams leave gaps. Email processing via SES, SFTP file ingestion via Transfer Family, IoT telemetry via IoT Core, SaaS data sync via AppFlow — each of these brings external, potentially untrusted data across your trust boundary. They must be logged, validated, and monitored just like any other ingress path.

⚠️

The often-missed pattern: Email → SES → S3 → Lambda is an attack surface. An adversary who controls an email they know you're processing can potentially influence execution. Treat every service-initiated data path as a potential injection vector.

LAYER 06Egress That Becomes Ingress

The most advanced and underappreciated concept in cloud entry analysis: your outbound traffic can create inbound risk. A Lambda function calling an external API, if that API is compromised, can receive malicious callback payloads. A compromised DNS resolution can redirect internal requests. Security is not just about what enters — it's about what leaves and what returns.

Controls here include VPC Flow Logs with alerting, DNS Firewall for outbound domain filtering, Network Firewall for egress inspection, and strict, documented outbound security group rules.

◆ PART TWO ◆
Administrative / Management Plane

The Master Keys: How Identities Control Your Environment

Here's the truth that every modern security team has internalized and that auditors are increasingly testing for:

In a cloud-native world, an entry point is not just an IP address. It is an IAM role.

— The Modern Security Perimeter

If an attacker steals a developer's ~/.aws/credentials file, they have entered your environment — without touching a Load Balancer, without triggering a WAF rule, without appearing in any network flow log. That is Entry Point #0.

ADMIN 01Console / CLI / SDK Access

This is the master key entry. Every human with the ability to call AWS APIs — whether through the Console, the CLI, or an SDK — is an administrative entry point. The required controls aren't suggestions: MFA enforced for every identity, SSO-only access through IAM Identity Center (no long-lived IAM users with permanent credentials), Service Control Policies applied organization-wide, and CloudTrail enabled in every region without exception.

When an auditor asks "how do you prevent privilege escalation?" — you need a clear, specific answer. "We have IAM" is not that answer. "We have Permission Boundaries attached to all roles, SCPs preventing IAM:CreateRole outside our provisioning pipeline, and Access Analyzer continuously reviewing cross-account permissions" is.

ADMIN 02Cross-Account Role Assumption

Assuming roles across accounts is one of AWS's most powerful patterns — and one of its most dangerous if misconfigured. A misconfigured trust policy with a wildcarded Principal is a door into your account left visibly open. Controls include External IDs for all cross-account assumptions, session duration limits appropriate to the use case, least-privilege policies reviewed quarterly, and full CloudTrail visibility on every AssumeRole event.

ADMIN 03Resource-Based Policy Exposure

S3 bucket policies, Lambda invoke permissions, KMS key policies, SQS queue policies, SNS topic policies — any resource-based policy with "Principal": "*" is potentially a public door. The controls that catch these: S3 Block Public Access enforced at the Organization level, IAM Access Analyzer running continuously with findings routed to your security team, and AWS Config rules alerting on any non-compliant resource policies.

◆ GOTCHA ◆
The "Stumper" Questions

Phase 3: What Auditors Ask to Test Depth

Technical screenshots alone don't pass SOC 2 audits. Experienced auditors probe the process behind the tooling. These are the three questions that separate teams who have built security culture from teams who installed security tools.

STUMPER 01
"Show me a 'Deny' rule in your logs."

This question tests whether your controls are actually doing anything. Have a CloudWatch Logs Insights query ready that filters WAF blocked requests or Security Group "REJECT" entries in VPC Flow Logs. Walking an auditor through a real blocked attack attempt — even a routine automated scan — proves your perimeter is working, not just configured.

STUMPER 02
"How do you know if an S3 bucket is made public right now — at this exact moment?"

This tests your continuous compliance posture versus point-in-time screenshots. Show them AWS Config Rule s3-bucket-public-read-prohibited in a non-compliant state triggering an EventBridge alert, which routes to your security team within minutes. The key word in their question is "right now" — your answer needs to demonstrate real-time detection, not a monthly audit.

STUMPER 03
"How do you handle 'ghost' entries from offboarded employees?"

This is the identity lifecycle question. Show your SSO/SCIM sync settings between your Identity Provider (Okta, Azure AD, Google Workspace) and IAM Identity Center. When an employee is disabled in the source IDP, their ability to assume any role in any AWS account is severed automatically — often within seconds. The absence of manual steps is the answer. Manual deprovisioning is a control gap; automated SCIM sync is a control.

💡

Pro tip for audit walkthroughs: Don't just show the tool — show the process. If you show an auditor a WAF dashboard, they will ask "Who reviews these logs, and how often?" If you show them an IAM role, they will ask "When was this last reviewed?" Have your Quarterly Access Review calendar invites and meeting minutes ready alongside every technical screenshot. Controls without process evidence are just claims.

Final Perspective

The High-Security Building

There's a useful mental model for all of this that's worth keeping visible in your architecture reviews.

Application Plane

The front doors, loading docks, and partner entrances. Every external entity — users, partners, IoT, SaaS — enters here. The building's security staff (WAF, Security Groups, ALB rules) decides who gets in and what they can carry.

Management Plane

The master key vault. Whoever holds these keys can open any room, modify any system, and rewrite the security rules themselves. This is where most breaches originate — and where most organizations under-invest.

Most companies secure the doors. They have WAF rules, they have Security Groups, they have Load Balancers in the right subnets. They can answer the auditor's question about external entry.

Mature companies secure the keys. They have SCIM-synced SSO, they have SCPs, they have Permission Boundaries, they have CloudTrail with real-time alerting on privilege escalation. They can answer the question about administrative entry — and they can prove the answers hold true at any moment in time.

Secure both.
Document both.
And you won't just pass audits —
you'll design architectures that deserve to.
